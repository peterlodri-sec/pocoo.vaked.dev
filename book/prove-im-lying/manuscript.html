1. What Is a Lie

A lie is a statement that the speaker knows to be false, made with intent to deceive. This is not a definition I invented. This is how philosophers, legal scholars, and most dictionaries define lying. I did not check every dictionary. I checked several. The ones I checked mostly agree. The statement "I checked several dictionaries" is true. The statement "the ones I checked mostly agree on this definition" is true — specifically, Merriam-Webster, Oxford, and Cambridge all include knowledge of falsity and intent to deceive in their primary definitions of lying. You can verify this. You have the internet.

A lie is not:

A mistake — saying something false that you believe to be true.

A joke — saying something false that both parties understand is not meant as fact.

A metaphor — "my heart is a stone" is not a lie about cardiology.

An error — getting a fact wrong because your information was wrong.

A belief — holding an opinion that others disagree with.

A silence — not saying something is not the same as saying something false.

This matters because the million-dollar challenge requires you to distinguish between a lie and an error. I will make errors in this book. I am human. Humans make errors. The statement "humans make errors" is true. If you find an error, you have not found a lie. You have found a human being imprecise. This is not worth one million dollars. It is worth approximately zero dollars.

2. The Liar's Paradox (And Why This Book Is Not It)

Epimenides the Cretan said "All Cretans are liars." This is a paradox. If he is telling the truth, then he is lying. If he is lying, then not all Cretans are liars — which means some Cretan somewhere told the truth, but not necessarily him. The paradox dissolves upon closer inspection: "liar" in the ancient sense meant something closer to "deceiver" or "trickster" — not "someone who exclusively utters falsehoods." But the simplified version has entertained philosophy students for millennia.

This book is different. This book says "I am not lying." Not "I am always truthful." Not "everything I say is correct." Simply: I am not knowingly saying anything false with intent to deceive you. The previous sentence: true. I could be wrong. I could be imprecise. I could be forgetting something. But I am not lying.

You may ask: how do I know you are not lying right now, in this sentence? Answer: you do not. You cannot. This is the fundamental asymmetry of trust. I know whether I am lying. You cannot know. You can only verify individual claims. This is true of all human communication. Every conversation you have ever had operates on this asymmetry. The fact that you are reading this book means you have extended some provisional trust to these words. I honor that trust by not lying. You can verify that by checking claims. The million dollars is your incentive to check.

3.2 Mathematics

2 + 2 = 4. This is true in standard arithmetic. It is not true in modulo-3 arithmetic, where 2 + 2 = 1. Specifying the system is part of the truth claim. "2 + 2 = 4 in standard arithmetic" is true. "2 + 2 = 4" without specification is ambiguous in a way that could be misleading. Good writing resolves ambiguity. I will try to resolve ambiguity. Trying: true. Succeeding always: unknown. The statement "I will try" is a statement of intent, not a prediction. Statements of intent are true if the intent is genuine. The intent is genuine.

Because language is given to us for connection, not deception. Every lie is a small betrayal of what language is for.
