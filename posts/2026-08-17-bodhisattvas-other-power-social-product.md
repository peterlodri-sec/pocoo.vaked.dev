# Bodhisattvas, the other power, and the social product of salvation
*qwave · buddhism · fine touch from within · vaked.dev*

In the sixth thesis of *The German Ideology* (1845–1846), Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels formulated an insight that permanently severed modern philosophy from Cartesian interiority: *"Consciousness is, therefore, from the very beginning a social product, and remains so as long as men exist at all."* Thirteen years later, in the 1859 *Preface to A Contribution to the Critique of Political Economy*, Marx compressed this structural principle into its canonical form: *"It is not the consciousness of men that determines their existence, but, on the contrary, their social existence that determines their consciousness."* If consciousness is not an autonomous, self-generating substance locked inside the skull of a sovereign individual, but the relational reflection of a shared social reality, then a severe soteriological problem immediately arises: **liberation cannot be a private enterprise.**

To imagine that the individual can, through heroic introspective exertion, pull itself out of the swamp of alienation by its own hair is to commit the fundamental bourgeois error—what Marx termed a *Robinsonade*, the myth of the self-sufficient actor producing value in total isolation.

Centuries before historical materialism diagnosed the social constitution of the human subject, Mahāyāna Buddhism arrived at an analogous crisis through its doctrine of *anātman* (non-self) and *pratītyasamutpāda* (dependent co-arising). If the ego is an empty illusion constituted entirely by dependent relations, how can an ego liberate itself? The radical answer to this paradox was not found in the quietist monasteries of the aristocracy, but in the Pure Land (*Jōdo*) movement of medieval Japan—culminating in the thought of Shinran (1173–1263). Shinran’s doctrine of *tariki* (他力, "Other Power") represents the religious form of the materialist thesis: **salvation is not an individual moral achievement, but an objective relational product.**

## 1. The Bodhisattva Ideal: From Solitary Arhatship to the Socialization of Merit

The historical foundation of this relational revolution lies in the evolution of the bodhisattva ideal. In early Buddhism (*Nikāya* / Theravāda), the term *bodhisatta* (Pali) possessed a restricted biographical scope. It referred almost exclusively to Siddhartha Gautama in his previous lives, beginning with the ascetic Sumedha prostrating in the mud before Dīpaṃkara Buddha and receiving the prophecy (*vyākaraṇa*) of future Buddhahood, or to the future Buddha Maitreya (Metteyya). The vast narrative corpus of the *Jātaka* tales documented these eons of heroic moral development, but the normative goal for the contemporary practitioner was the *arhat*—the solitary saint who extinguishes personal defilements (*kleśas*) to exit the cycle of saṃsāra.

The Mahāyāna turn fundamentally dismantled this individualist soteriology. The etymology of *bodhisattva* (*bodhi*, awakening; *sattva*, being, essence, or spiritual resolve; translated into Tibetan as *byang chub sems dpa'*, where *sems dpa'* signifies a spiritual warrior or heroic mind) was expanded into an all-encompassing ethical category. Awakening was stripped of its private character. The arising of *bodhicitta*—the resolve to attain enlightenment for the sake of all sentient beings without exception—became the indispensable gateway. 

Under the banner of *mahākaruṇā* (great compassion) and *upāya-kauśalya* (skillful means), the bodhisattva’s path introduced what classical texts paradoxically framed as "delayed enlightenment" (or more precisely, non-abiding nirvana, *apratiṣṭhita-nirvāṇa*). The bodhisattva refuses to enter private extinction while a single being remains trapped in suffering.

Crucially, this shift reconfigured the metaphysical economy of karma. In early Buddhist scholasticism, karma was strictly privatized: one’s actions generated personal karmic fruit (*phala*), which could only be experienced by the agent. Mahāyāna introduced the revolutionary mechanism of *pariṇāmanā* (merit transference; Japanese: *ekō*, 回向). The bodhisattva accumulates vast stores of merit (*puṇya*) through ascetic discipline and insight, only to redistribute that spiritual surplus outward to all beings. Merit ceased to be private property; it became a collective fund. The state of non-retrogression (*avaivartika*) was achieved precisely when the practitioner ceased clinging to personal liberation and became a conduit for universal deliverance.

## 2. Amitābha and the Architecture of Sukhāvatī

The radicalization of merit transfer reached its zenith in the cult of Amitābha (*Amita-ābha*, Immeasurable Light) and Amitāyus (*Amita-āyus*, Immeasurable Life). While early epigraphic traces emerge in Gandhara as early as the second century CE (notably the Govindnagar pedestal inscription dated to c. 153 CE during the Kushan era), the theological architecture was codified in three foundational texts: the *Larger Sukhāvatīvyūha Sūtra*, the *Smaller Sukhāvatīvyūha Sūtra*, and the *Guan Wuliangshou jing* (*Contemplation Sūtra*).

The narrative mythos of the *Larger Sūtra* centers on the monk Dharmākara (Japanese: *Hōzō Bosatsu*). Before the Buddha Lokeśvararāja, Dharmākara surveyed countless Buddha-fields for five kalpas, synthesizing their finest qualities into forty-eight specific vows (*pūrvapraṇidhāna*). His vow was an ontological wager: he refused to accept final Buddhahood unless his supreme land of bliss, Sukhāvatī, was created as an easily accessible realm wherein any being could attain final awakening without regressing.

Among these vows, the **18th Vow**—revered in the Pure Land tradition as the *Primal Vow* (*Hongan*, 本願)—contains the core democratic rupture:

> *"If, when I attain Buddhahood, sentient beings in the lands of the ten directions who sincerely and joyfully entrust themselves to me, desire to be born in my land, and think of me even ten times should not be born there, may I not attain perfect enlightenment."*

![Sukhavati relief](/assets/bodhisattva/western-paradise.jpg)

The *Contemplation Sūtra* deepened this accessibility through a stark narrative of human crisis. Set against the royal parricide of Rajagriha, Queen Vaidehī—imprisoned and devastated by her son Ajātaśatru—cries out to Śākyamuni for a world free of corruption. The Buddha presents sixteen contemplative visualizations (*shisō*) of Sukhāvatī, which culminate in the taxonomy of the "nine grades of rebirth" (*kuhon ōjō*). 

The lowest grade of the lowest class (*gebon geshō*) specifically addresses murderers, thieves, and practitioners of the five grave offenses (*gogyaku*): beings utterly incapable of meditation, visualization, or moral restraint. For them, simply reciting the name of Amitābha ten times on their deathbed ensures immediate rebirth in Sukhāvatī upon the lotus pedestal.

The famous *Taima Mandala* (*Taima Mandara*), based on the *Contemplation Sūtra*, visually organizes this cosmic geography: the opulent court of Amitābha flanked by Avalokiteśvara and Mahāsthāmaprāpta at the center, flanked on the margins by Vaidehī’s tragedy and the nine tiers of descent. 

The structural implication is unmistakable: **the Pure Land is not earned.** It is not an alpine summit scaled by athletic ascetics. It is an objective environmental reality constructed by the cosmic surplus merit of *another* being’s vow, made available without cost to the bankrupt.

## 3. Hōnen and the Exclusive Nembutsu: The Egalitarian Rupture

When Buddhism crossed into medieval Japan, it was captured by the aristocratic elite of the Heian court and the monastic fortresses of Mount Hiei (Tendai) and Mount Kōya (Shingon). Monastic ordination, esoteric rituals (*mikkyō*), textual literacy, and aristocratic sponsorship formed an impenetrable economic monopoly on salvation. Buddhism functioned as the ideological superstructure of the feudal court, legitimizing imperial power and extracting agrarian surpluses.

In 1175, the Tendai monk Hōnen (1133–1212) broke violently with this monastic orthodoxy. While reading the Tang-dynasty master Shandao’s (613–681) *Commentary on the Contemplation Sūtra*, Hōnen encountered a line that catalyzed his spiritual conversion: *"Only repeat the name of Amitābha with all your heart... this is the act of right determination."* 

Abandoning Mount Hiei, Hōnen founded the independent Jōdo-shū (Pure Land School) and authored the *Senchakushū* (*Senchaku Hongan Nembutsu Shū*, 1198). He proclaimed the practice of *senju nembutsu* (the exclusive recitation of *Namu Amida Butsu*).

![Raigo descent](/assets/bodhisattva/raigo-descent.jpg)

Hōnen’s doctrine stripped salvation of its material and institutional barriers. No meditation, no philosophical comprehension, no wealth, and no moral purity were required. As Richard Bowring observes in *The Religious Traditions of Japan*, the established monastic orders brought two devastating charges against Hōnen’s movement:
1. The exclusive *nembutsu* denied the validity of all other Buddhist practices, devaluing centuries of temple rituals, esoteric rites, and monastic endowments.
2. Hōnen *"puts the lowest lay person on the same level as the wisest monk."*

By opening salvation indiscriminately to samurai, merchants, prostitutes (*yūjo*), fishermen, and thieves—classes whose everyday labor violated Buddhist precepts against the taking of life or sexual purity—Hōnen threatened the socio-economic foundation of medieval ecclesiastical power. 

The monastic establishment struck back in the Jōgen Persecution of 1207: four of Hōnen’s disciples were executed, and Hōnen (along with his younger student Shinran) was defrocked, given a peasant name, and exiled to the remote provinces.

## 4. Shinran and Tariki: The Annihilation of Self-Power

If Hōnen democratized the *nembutsu*, it was his disciple Shinran (1173–1263) who pursued its philosophical and psychological logic to its absolute conclusion, creating Jōdo Shinshū (True Pure Land School). 

Exiled to the freezing coast of Echigo and later settling for twenty years among the agrarian peasantry of the Kantō region, Shinran discarded monastic identity altogether. Calling himself *Gutoku Shinran* ("Foolish, Bald-Headed Shinran"), he declared himself *hisō hizoku*—"neither monk nor layman." He married the nun Eshinni, raised children, and lived embedded in the material realities of peasant life. In his masterwork, the *Kyōgyōshinshō*, and the oral dialogues recorded by Yuien in the *Tannishō*, Shinran executed a radical theoretical deconstruction of *jiriki* (自力, "Self-Power").

Shinran’s primary breakthrough concerns *shinjin* (信心, true entrusting or faith). In conventional religion, faith is understood as an internal psychological exertion: the individual *decides* to believe, generating trust through willpower. Shinran realized that this subjective faith is merely another covert manifestation of *jiriki*—the ego’s desperate attempt to assert control over its own deliverance. 

Instead, Shinran argued that true *shinjin* is entirely non-originated within the human subject:
> *"shinjin in beings is itself the mind of Amida and that Amida gives his mind to beings... the nembutsu is not the invocation of the Buddha by beings but rather the Buddha's call to beings"* (SEP: Hirota 4.3).

The recitation of the *nembutsu* is not an accumulative spiritual work or a technique to buy divine favor. It is *"neither a religious practice nor a good act... without any calculation (hakarai)"* (*Tannishō* ch. 8). It is simply the voice of the Other Power echoing through the empty shell of the individual. 

Shinran grounded this in Shandao’s formulation of the "twofold deep mind" (*nishu jinshin*): the realization that one is a foolish being (*bonbu*) hopelessly mired in karmic bondage, accompanied by total reliance on the Primal Vow. Without the prior approach of Amida, this honest self-awareness is impossible; the ego cannot even recognize its own sickness without an external standard (SEP: Hirota 4.2).

![Shinran portrait](/assets/bodhisattva/shinran-portrait.jpg)

This inversion produced the famous paradoxical doctrine of *akunin shōki* (悪人正機, "the wicked person as the primary object of the Vow"):

> *"Even a virtuous person can attain rebirth, how much more easily a wicked person!"* (*Tannishō* ch. 3).

The "virtuous person" (*zenin*) relies on their accumulated moral credentials—their *jiriki*—and therefore maintains an illusion of self-sufficiency that resists the Vow. The "wicked person" (*akunin*), possessing zero spiritual capital, has nowhere to turn except the unearned grace of the Other.

Shinran’s institutional stance was equally radical. In chapter 6 of the *Tannishō*, he famously declared: *"I do not possess even a single disciple."* He refused pastoral authority, sectarian ownership, and spiritual hierarchy. If salvation is the working of Amida's Vow alone, there can be no masters and no disciples—only *dōbō* (fellow companions) and *dōgyō* (fellow travelers) walking the same path.

## 5. The Materialist Parallel: Consciousness and Grace as Relational Products

Here we can state our central philosophical synthesis: **Shinran’s *tariki* is the religious prefiguration of the materialist thesis.** 

Consider the precise structural correspondence between historical materialism and radical Pure Land ontology. In the classical bourgeois view of mind (from Descartes to Kant), the interior subject is sovereign, foundational, and self-explanatory. Value, morality, and understanding are generated from within the private Cartesian theater. 

Marx and Engels demolished this illusion. In the *German Ideology*, they demonstrated that language and thought arise only from the material necessity of intercourse with other humans. In the 1873 Afterword to *Capital* (Volume I), Marx wrote the formulation that V.I. Lenin famously highlighted in his *Philosophical Notebooks*: *"the ideal is nothing else than the material world reflected by the human mind, and translated into forms of thought."* The internal life of the individual is an emergent property of social relations, class structure, and material conditions.

```
       BOURGEOIS / MONASTIC MODEL                 MATERIALIST / TARIKI MODEL
       
   ┌───────────────────────────────┐          ┌────────────────────────────────┐
   │     Sovereign Ego / Monk      │          │        Social / Cosmic         │
   │                               │          │           Relations            │
   │  ┌─────────────────────────┐  │          └───────────────┬────────────────┘
   │  │   Private Accumulation  │  │                          │
   │  │  (Merit / Capital /     │  │                          ▼ (Constitutes)
   │  │   Spiritual Virtues)    │  │          ┌────────────────────────────────┐
   │  └────────────┬────────────┘  │          │   Individual Subject (Empty)   │
   │               │               │          │                                │
   │               ▼               │          │  * Consciousness = Social      │
   │     Salvation / Autonomy      │          │  * Shinjin = Gift of Other     │
   └───────────────────────────────┘          └────────────────────────────────┘
```

Mahāyāna philosophy had already established the ontological base through *śūnyatā* (emptiness) and *pratītyasamutpāda*: the ego-self (*ātman*) is a delusional linguistic construct without independent self-nature (*svabhāva*). But early monastic practice often contradicted this insight in practice, establishing an ascetic merit-economy where monks "earned" liberation through private heroic discipline.

Shinran recognized this fundamental contradiction. If the ego is truly non-existent, any spiritual practice that claims to achieve enlightenment through "Self-Power" (*jiriki*) is a performative self-contradiction. It attempts to use the illusory ego to dismantle the illusory ego. 

Shinran’s radical move was to relocate the locus of liberation entirely outside the monadic subject. Just as Marx showed that an individual cannot generate language or consciousness in a vacuum, Shinran showed that the individual cannot generate *shinjin* or liberation from within its own corrupted ego-calculations (*hakarai*). 

Salvation is constituted from a relation. The entrusting mind (*shinjin*) is not an internal psychological conquest; it is the imprint of an objective, external, trans-individual reality (the Primal Vow) entering the human consciousness. In both systems, the individual is decentralized: interiority is the consequence of an objective relational reality, never its cause.

*(Note: This synthesis represents an interpretive structural correspondence between historical materialism and Buddhist soteriology, rather than an identity of historical intent.)*

## 6. The Social Reading: From Akunin Shōki to Buddhist Socialism

This theological dismantling of private merit had immediate, revolutionary social consequences. 

In medieval Japan, who was the *akunin* (the "evil person")? In aristocratic Tendai ideology, the "evil" were not simply moral reprobates; they were the laboring masses whose material survival required violating religious taboos. Farmers who tilled the earth killed worms and insects; fishermen killed fish; butchers, leatherworkers, and hunters shed blood; warriors engaged in slaughter; prostitutes engaged in illicit trade. Aristocrats, by contrast, had the material wealth to live cleanly off tenant rents, fund temple construction, and pay monks to chant sutras on their behalf. 

*Akunin shōki* was therefore a veiled **class critique**. It announced that those who had been excluded from spiritual capital by the feudal division of labor were the primary target of universal liberation. The aristocratic merit-economy was exposed as a theological racket.

This egalitarian logic explains why Jōdo Shinshū quickly organized into horizontal, self-governing village communities called *kō* (講) and *dōjō*. By the 15th century, these autonomous peasant leagues coalesced into the *Ikkō-ikki* (一向一揆)—massive peasant uprisings that drove out feudal warlords (*daimyō*) and established autonomous, self-governing provinces in Kaga and elsewhere for nearly a century, prefiguring modern agrarian communes.

In the twentieth century, this subterranean connection between Pure Land egalitarianism and socialism erupted into explicit political movements:

* **Takagi Kenmyō (1864–1914):** A Shinshu priest from the discriminated *burakumin* class, Takagi was arrested and sentenced to death during the High Treason Incident of 1910 (*Taigyaku Jiken*). In his prison treatise, *My Socialism* (*Waga Shakaishugi*), Takagi explicitly fused Shinran’s thought with socialist struggle: *"The Land of Bliss is the place in which socialism is truly practiced... Amida Buddha's Vow makes no distinction between the noble and the base, the rich and the poor."*
* **Senoo Girō (1889–1961):** Founder of the Youth League for Revitalizing Buddhism (*Shinkō Bukkyō Seinen Dōmei*, 1931), Senoo argued that capitalist accumulation was a direct manifestation of Buddhist greed (*lobha*) and structural theft (*adinnādānā*), calling for a non-violent socialist revolution rooted in Mahāyāna collectivism.
* **B. R. Ambedkar (1891–1956):** In his historic 1956 treatise *The Buddha or Karl Marx*, the architect of the Indian Dalit Buddhist movement analyzed the convergence and divergence of both systems, concluding that while Marx provided the structural diagnosis of exploitation, the Buddhist *Dhamma* provided the essential ethical and psychological foundation of fraternity (*maitrī*) without which social equality collapses into dictatorship.
* **The 14th Dalai Lama (Tenzin Gyatso):** Reflecting on this legacy in the late 20th century, he famously declared himself *"half-Marxist, half-Buddhist,"* praising Marxism’s focus on wealth distribution and internationalism while grounding it in Buddhist compassion.

## 7. Objections, Limits, and Intellectual Honesty

To maintain intellectual rigor, we must clearly delineate the limits of this analogy. A materialist critique must resist the temptation of superficial syncretism:

| Dimension | Historical Materialism (Marx/Engels) | Pure Land Soteriology (Shinran) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Ontological Domain** | Material-social relations, labor, economic production. | Cosmic Vow (*Hongan*), metaphysical Buddha-field, trans-historical grace. |
| **Agency of Change** | Collective class struggle, political praxis, historical development. | Total abandonment of self-calculation (*hakarai*), receptive entrustment (*shinjin*). |
| **Telos** | Classless communist society realized on historical earth. | Rebirth in Sukhāvatī, leading to non-retrograde Buddhahood. |

The parallel between Marx’s "social product" and Shinran’s "Other Power" is a **structural isomorphism**, not an ontological identity. Marx operates within the realm of material immanence; Shinran operates within the realm of transcendental religious symbolism.

Furthermore, Ambedkar offered a crucial immanent critique of orthodox Marxism: reductive economism fails to account for the autonomy of psychological and spiritual alienation. Changing the ownership of the means of production does not automatically eradicate greed, hatred, and delusion (*the three poisons*). Religion is not merely "the opium of the people"—it can also be, as Marx himself conceded in that same passage, the *"heart of a heartless world."* The Buddhist claim is that relationality not only conditions consciousness, but holds the key to its existential liberation.

Finally, Shinran himself was acutely aware of the danger of vulgar antinomianism—what the tradition calls *zōaku mugai* (造悪無碍, "licensed evil," or deliberately committing sins because Amida saves wicked people). In *Tannishō* (Chapter 13), Shinran decisively rejected this distortion: *"Do not take poison just because there is an antidote."* To deliberately perform evil in order to qualify for grace is merely another insidious form of *jiriki*—a calculated, self-centered effort to manipulate the Primal Vow.

As the Swiss Protestant theologian Karl Barth astutely observed in his *Church Dogmatics*, Jōdo Shinshū represents the most rigorous and pure Eastern "religion of grace" (SEP: Hirota 1.2). And grace, whether formulated in the theological language of the 13th-century Pure Land or the materialist language of 19th-century political economy, shares an immutable core truth: **it is precisely that which the isolated individual cannot manufacture for itself.**

If consciousness is a social product, then the illusion of the self-made individual is the root of both spiritual suffering and economic exploitation. Salvation is not a trophy won by the sovereign ego, but an awakening to the infinite relational web that sustained us long before we learned to calculate our own worth.
